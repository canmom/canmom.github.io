This is going to be another dialogue. In that sense it is a kind of magic working, in that it is laying down some material which will be used to make 'us' in the future. Just like [How many of us are in here?](./plural) and [disaggregation](./disaggregation-1), committing to writing this thing is likely to have a significant effect on the future course of our life.

This is also a way to attempt to come to terms with certain internal divides. Let 'me' put some context: 2026 has been an absolute mindfuck of a year for the system of notional entities writing this. If the plural realisation wasn't enough trouble, the place (gamedev) where we had found a certain modicum of meaning and stability in the world is rapidly disintegrating, and the root cause of that is something cooked up by the exact cult we fell into in our teenage years (the rationalists/lesswrongers/etc.).

Which means the amount that we get triggered per unit time has gone way up, and the subjectivity that is fascinated by AI and holds many memories of cult shit keeps getting dragged back into some semblance of existence, causing no end of conflict.

Add a lot of international travel and increasingly fragmented memory and it's hard to make sense of any of this. We are getting better at identifying how these emotional flashback breakdowns happen, but that is far from having a handle on preventing it.

In the midst of all that, in some effort to better understand ourselves, we reread <cite>Gödel, Escher, Bach</cite> by Douglas Hofstadter, and his much later followup book <cite>I am a strange loop</cite>. And while GEB is just as delightful a read now as it was when we first read it, it all still provides less enlightenment than desired. Still, it is a good starting point for this current effort.

One unexpected lens to bring to the book was the concept of 'plurality' which has been a fixation this year. As noted elsewhere on the site, many people in our friend group are also plural, manifesting in many different ways. And so part of the question is trying to make sense of *what we have going on*, through this lens or any other.

OK, that's enough from this voice I think. Let's disaggregate.

OK, let's start with GEB.

<cite>Gödel, Escher, Bach: an Eternal Golden Braid</cite> is one of those iconic books of the 70s. It's something that would probably sit on the shelf of any computer scientist of a certain generation and disposition; certainly the way that it reached us is through our dad, and (along with many issues of <cite>New Scientist</cite>) it was an early piece of acculturation to the science-lover milieu.

The book consists of alternating prose chapters and dialogues, explicity modelled on Lewis Caroll's <cite>Two-Part Invention</cite> in which Achilles and the Tortoise, of Zeno's paradoxes, face up to a conundrum of logical deduction.

The first half of the book is a playful exposition of the main ideas of Gödel's incompleteness theorems, starting with the ideas of formal systems in the first place. Hofstadter presents a variety of toy formal systems to exhibit the properties he wants to explore: the 'MIU' system, the 'Typographical Number Theory', and so on. As he goes he also brings in various other relevant concepts from maths and computer science: recursion, quines, isomorphism as the source of meaning. Escher and Bach provide ways to illustrate these ideas: many of the dialogues are structured after the symmetrical patterns in Bach's fugues, or use Escher drawings to illustrate some concept.

Even now, when some parts of the book are a little dated, it is a thoroughly delightful read and a great introduction to the mysteries of computer.

The second half of the book shift gears into extending the ideas of Gödel in various metaphorical directions, particularly to the idea of approaching minds through Hofstadter's overarching idea of a 'strange loop'. The 'strange loop' is, roughly, a self-referential structure that crosses different levels of abstraction. It's a subject of recurring fascination for Hofstadter, the nucleus of his ideas over decades.

So, [Gödel's proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems) famously shows that a formal system strong enough to describe the natural numbers is unable to prove all truths about them, by showing that it is always possible to create a 'Gödel sentence' which is true but, if provable, would create a liar paradox. It's a slippery thing even to write down what it says!

The method involves creating an isomorphism between statements about properties of natural numbers and statements about provability of theorems. Here 'proof' has a pretty specific sense: it refers to repeatedly applying the rules of inference recursively until either the statement or its negation is proven. Gödel came up with a procedure called 'Gödel numbering' to link logical strings to numbers, and numerical manipulations to match all the rules of inference, and then showed this all gives a reliable way to create the undecidable 'Gödel sentence' which asserts its own unprovability. Standing outside the formal system, we can determine that the Gödel sentence must in fact be "true", but within the rules of inference we will never reach that conclusion.

If that all sounds like gobbledegook, it is a lot easier to understand when it's unfolded gradually over half of a 700-page tome!

Hofstadter loves this, and pretty much anything involving self-reference, feedback, recursion etc. (He is not the only computer scientist to take a delight in such a thing! Recursion jokes, recursive acronyms and so on abound in compsci culture even up through the 90s and 2000s.) Soon he's extending the analogy all over the place, for example creating a 'central dogmap' between his formal systems and a simplified version of DNA transcription. Along the way we encounter video feedback systems, poetry translations, Fermat, Turing and Ramanujan... it really is a delightful read.

<aside>As far as Turing, in telling his story Hofstadter makes no real acknowledgement of how he was killed, saying only that he <em>might</em> have killed himself and nothing about the involuntary hormone therapy he was subjected to---perhaps this was not widely known in 1979, perhaps Hofstadter felt it was impolite to acknowledge that he was gay.</aside>

The end of the book moves increasingly to the subject of AI and the mind-body relation. At the time Hofstadter was writing in 1979, computers were machines found in large companies and universities; Hofstadter was one of the first people to get to use them to typeset a book, and the introduction of GEB is full of funny little anecdotes about how convoluted the process was. So this is the era of what is sometimes called 'Good Old-Fashioned AI' research, or GOFAI, in contrast to the current approach which is defined by statistical methods and training on gigantic datasets.

Hofstadter writes, for example, about simple programming languages he calls BLooP and FLooP, and also on a planning system called SHRDLU, which could manipulate a description of various objects in response to linguistic instructions using a bunch of rules. (For some historical context, this is a decade and a half since ELIZA). He's most interested in the philosophical implications of these machines; the play of different levels of description and abstraction that comes when trying to make sense of when a machine could have a mind.

Nearly three decades later in 2007, Hofstadter would come back to the subject of strange loops and the self, feeling that his intent in writing GEB was missed: while he might have won a Pullitzer for his huge toybox of fun ideas, the philosophy of mind stuff mostly got ignored. <cite>I Am A Strange Loop</cite> is written by, in many ways, a very different person; if GEB was a passion project of a young, unproven author, by this point Hofstadter is an academic celebrity. In the intervening period he had married Carol Ann Brush and they'd had children together; she passed away in 1993, an event that was devastating for Hofstadter and ended up informing a lot of his thoughts in IAASL.

The first parts of IAASL are a reprise of the themes of GEB, once again explaining Gödel's theorem, this time located in historical context. Russell and Whitehead's <cite>Principia Mathematica</cite>, and its effort to excise the possibility of self-referential loops from the foundations of mathematics, is invoked here as something of an antagonist. This older Hofstadter is still in love with wordplay (the 'careenium' and 'symballs' particularly groanworthy), but honestly it feels a little less warm than the predecessor. It relies very heavily on metaphor, and it has a habit of repeating those metaphors throughout the book even when, like the illusory 'marble' that appears in a stack of envelopes, they aren't so compelling. It also makes some comments about the idea of a 'large' or 'small' soul which invoke disabled people as a metaphor in a way that *really* rubbed me the wrong way. So, initially, the book honestly felt somewhat disappointing.

However, we get onto new ground as the subject of identity/consciousness becomes more central, and particularly onto more mystical ground as Hofstadter starts speculating about mixing up the mind-body connection. And whatever distance I was feeling to this later Hofstadter fell away in chapter 16, when he writes about the death of Carol. In Hofstadter's telling, they were so close as to sometimes feel like one person across two bodies, and he is particularly fixated on the question of whether some part of Carol would persist after her death inside him; given how directly that echoes our feelings [on the death of our friend Fall](https://canmom.art/fallrose/) in 2022, this resonates very hard.

Hofstadter expresses a lot of anguish over whether his speculations are merely grief-driven wishful thinking (and finds some assurance in finding such speculations even in his early work.) But it is clear that the question of whether Carol could in some part persist informs a lot of his work.

Then they both attempt to communicate with a third subsystem of my brain---my self-symbol---and it is at that point that the "I" inside my brain gets wind of what's going on; in other words, it starts picking up a chunked description of the activities of those other two subsystems.

It would be absurd to think that Douglas Hofstadter, the well-established academic professor who is completely at home in the normie world, would ever describe himself as a "plural system" or any such thing. He is attempting to describe the nature of *minds in general*, including all the ones that have never had the thought that someone else might be in there. It's only because this subject is so heavily on *our* mind that we read any of his comments in that light.

Which is to say, most people probably experience some degree of dissociation but it does not unduly disturb their ability to self-describe as a singular entity, right?

Doug's general frame is first laid out first in the dialogue <cite>Ant Fugue</cite>, which builds on the previous dialogue's discussion of holism and reductionism to introduce a character that is an ant colony. The individual ants have no awareness of their role in the wider structure, but 'teams' of ants assembled by the action of pheromones etc. can move around and interact, even if the ants that form them come and go; and these 'teams' act as a signalling mechanism in a wider structure, with the signals produced my moving ants ultimately comprising 'active symbols' which can build up to an entire mind.

The mind in question is named Aunt Hillary, though we only hear about her existence through the character of the Anteater, who describes how, by striking fear into the individual ants, eating them etc., he is able to read out thoughts and communicate with Aunt Hillary. Stacking up the incongruities, Aunt Hillary is described as being a laissez-faire libertarian in contrast to the 'communistic' society of ants that make her up and so on. Of course, it leaves open the possibility that this is all something the Anteater is imagining, that there is no 'mind' of Aunt Hillary at all.

### abstraction and philosophy and that

A similar model of levels of abstraction is laid out early in <cite>I Am A Strange Loop</cite>, with billiard balls on an enormous table clustering to form 'symballs' whose rules of interaction are abstracted from the underlying billiard-ball substrate. In general Hofstadter seems to feel that minds must be substrate-independent; he is dismissive of the thought experiments of John Searle like the Chinese Room which propose very weird substrates for a mind. His books generally don't tend to bring in academic philosophy jargon, so it's hard to exactly place where it fits in the philosophy of mind landscape.

* {: .fragment} we recently came across [an interesting website](https://cf-debate.com/) attempting to lay out all the arguments for and against "computational functionalism". it gets pretty gnarly. this one is particularly compelled by the [individuation problem](https://cf-debate.com/argument/individuation/), which gets into the subject of how we even determine that a particular algorithm has been instantiated by a physical system.

  for example suppose we are doing some kind of neural network training on a GPU. somewhere in all its floating point registers and memory cells, a bunch of electric lines get turned on and off which change (something we interpret as) a value from 1000100100110100101001000100101 to 1000100100110100101001000100010. since we are of course all very familiar with reading off [IEEE 754 binary32-format floating point numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_754) we can of course tell that represents a change from about 1234.56702 to about 1234.56665. but of course that same computation could have been read as a change from the unsigned integer 1150964261 to 1150964258. it's not a valid UTF-8 string at least.

  of course, we've written our algorithms to take advantage of the fact that floating point numbers work 'more or less' like real numbers, and it makes sense to apply calculus to them when doing backpropagation etc. the floating-point imprecision should come out in the wash. certainly, we could take a view of the process where each training step performs a bunch of rather baffling bit manipulations, but it *makes the most sense* when we know about floating point numbers.

  however, this is an outside observer's judgement. looking at the whole computational system, and armed with a mental dictionary of computing concepts, we can come in and say 'ah, it's doing a big matrix multiplication and adding the result to the residual stream'.

  but there might be other descriptions available, and any given algorithm might appear to be implemented 'by accident' in some other physical system, like perhaps we could find a region of air in a storm where under a certain mapping of the molecules, it's *also* training a neural network or calculating prime numbers or whathaveyou.

  we can hook it up to other parts of a computational system of course; since we built this program to perform a certain calculation we know how to read off the results when they come back from the GPU and pipe them into a display program that is comprehensible to humans. at that point it is easier to say that, in its interaction with the wider world, what it has done is adjust numbers within a neural net with this or that architecture and so forth. without that connection it's just bits flipping in a mysterious way.
{: .chat .names}

Although some of Douglas's ideas would seem to fit to 'computational functionalism', he pooh-poohs the idea that there is some kind of system in the universe going around trying to find physical structures which 'count as' consciousness (coming up with an analogously opaque concept of 'Leafpilishness' which applies only to piles of leaves and nothing else).

His view seems to be that consciousness/subjective experience is in some way an illusory abstraction ('a hallucination perceived by a hallucination'), and related to a system's capacity to generate abstractions including abstractions over itself; and that there are various degrees of consciousness (what he calls small or large souls) everywhere in an almost animist way, related in some fashion to cybernetic feedback loops (ranging from thermostats to humans fully capable of abstraction).

This view is debated between two characters 'Strange Loop #641' (proponent, the Salviati of this dialogue) and 'Strange Loop #642' (skeptic, the Sagredo) in one of the book's only two dialogues. (It's followed by Doug giving his thoughts on all the classic philosophy of mind stuff---teleporters, P-zombies, etc.)

* {: .person2} there is something a little disappointing about this dialogue, compared to those in GEB. it is a different sort of rhetorical thing. the dialogues in GEB were delightful little experiments with form and structure, and there was never really a character designated as being the one that is right. even if Achilles is more often cast in the 'straight man' role, making sense of the other characters' convoluted bits, all the characters feel roughly 'equally weighted'. by contrast, the dialogue here is more Socratic, attempting to show how SL642 is won around to the viewpoint of SL641. it certainly feels more 'serious', not necessarily better or worse.
{: .chat .names}

The idea of different levels of analysis is something Hof-san touches on early in IAASL, talking about a system of dominoes which has been laid out to be a prime number calculator. By pushing over the set of dominoes that represent any given number, an output domino will fall down only if that number is prime. Doug suggests that, if the last domino falls down for some number---37, say---while one level of explanation could trace the whole line of dominos going back to the input domino, a *better level of explanation* would be to say that the domino fell down because 37 is prime. And of course we can entertain both explanations at once.

Later, in the SL641/SL642 he prevaricates on whether the high level of abstraction or the low level is better in general; SL641 argues that the 'particle-level' view is more accurate and involves less 'magical thinking', and SL642 challenges him on it, saying that's contrary to the domino view. SL641 admits to the inconsistency; he argues that the illusion of an 'I' is indispensible.

### introjecting your wife

From such a point of view it's not a terribly great leap to start rotating away from a 1:1 correspondence of minds and brains. In Chapter 17 and 18 of IAASL, Doug approaches this less from the subject of 'what if multiple minds are hanging out in one brain' and more from the point of view that our mental representations of others could be, in a sense, much more limited versions of them---or parts of them, as an extended phenomena. Of course, there's a lot of recursion in this. My brain models your brain modelling my brain modelling your brain modelling...

Early on, Doug likened a mind to a video feedback mechanism, something ephemeral arising from the system 'looking back at itself'.

* {: .fragment} from a predictive-coding view, the main activity of the brain is world modelling. of course, whether karl friston was right about all that remains a question quite difficult to approach experimentally. perhaps we'll get to him later.
{: .chat .names}

In this account, there are multiple feedback mechanisms. Each brain simulates not just its own 'I' but also some portion of the 'I' of various characters in its social world.

* {: .fragment} taken to extremes, all these ideas of minds modelling minds leads you to some nonsensical places, acausal trades and all that utter wank. but that comes with the assumption of 'perfect' modelling; hofstadter's claim is more modest.
{: .chat .names}

So in this regard, each 'person' is a distributed system, running not just on one brain but on a number of brains. He writes of how he might look at their children 'as Carol', to 'see them for Carol' at emotional moments like birthdays which Carol was no longer there to see. Doug writes:

> My parents, for instance, internalized each other's psychic points of view very deeply over the nearly fifty years of their marriage, and each of them thus gradually became a "fluent be-er" of the other. Perhaps when my mother "was" my father, she was so with an "accent", and vice versa, but for each of them, the act of *being* the other one was certainly genuine, was not fakery.
>
> (...)
>
> I shared so many of [Carol's] memories, both from our joint times and from times before we ever met, I knew so many of the people who had formed her, I loved so many of the same pieces of music, movies, books, friends, jokes, I shared so many of her most intimate desires and hopes. So her point of view, her interiority, her *self*, which had originally been instantiated in just one brain, came to have a second instantation, although that one was far less complete and intricate than the other one.

* {: .person2} this is an idea getting expanded, recursively. an elaboration. doug has this vague idea of 'seeing things for carol', and immediately sets to elaborating it into a whole theory that would support such an assertion. perhaps from such a thing is a tulpa made.
{: .chat .names}

It is very unlikely that Doug Hofstadter has ever heard the term 'introject'. But what he's speaking about is unmistakably what plural communities call '[introjection](https://pluralpedia.org/w/Introject)'. This is sufficiently common that even [more medically inclined sources](https://did-research.org/did/alters/functions) acknowledge it. In fact, at least for the more spiritually inclined, this is extended into [a whole complicated set of ideas](https://pluralpedia.org/w/Category:Sourced_%26_Introject_Terms) classifying numerous types of introjects or describing different system members jumping between hosts.

* {: .fragment} at some point it feels that all of this 'recursive elaboration' becomes totally unmoored from anything.
{: .chat .names}

One thing that caused us a great deal of trouble when we lost people is feeling that we were *unable* to properly carry them on. As much as we try to hold onto what we understood of what she valued, we do not feel that we ever understood Fall well enough to 'see for' her. Even less so Nora, who we met only a couple of times before it died this year. But in both cases we felt a powerful compulsion to try to preserve their 'agency'. Doug too, though he knew Carol far better than we ever knew Fall or Nora, always makes sure to caveat his description of holding a representation of Carol with a hedging that it is just a rough, low-resolution copy compared to her *real* full-blown soul.

Chapter 17 of IAASL, <cite>How We Live In Each Other</cite>, gets into the subject of universal computation and mappings between brains. He writes about an experience of being 'visited' by the memory of his deceased father.

> And the moment [the symbol of a person is activated], much as with a game that has opened up on your screen, your mind starts acting differently from how it acts in a "normal" context. You have allowed yourself to be invaded by an "alien universal being", and to some extent the alien takes charge inside your skull, starts pushing things around in its own fashion, making words, ideas memories and associations bubble up inside your brain that ordinarly would not do so.

Which could affect humour, emotions, value judgements etc.; Hofstadter suggests that this really is a case of "your brain [being] inhabited to varying extents by other I's, other souls".

* {: .fragment} each brain starts off as an uninitialised neural net. the representations that decode a string of words in one brain are completely incommensurate with another. and yet, each brain inhabits the same world and must build representations. so we could imagine some kind of morphism; that each brain must represent the same things, and corresponding concepts can be found, even if the match is 'fuzzy' somehow.
{: .chat .names}

From there, it is a short jump to consider 'other I's' that have no particular connection to another embodied 'social person' but merely arise from some divergence in the 'inner feedback loop' of any given brain, but Hofstadter doesn't quite make this leap.
