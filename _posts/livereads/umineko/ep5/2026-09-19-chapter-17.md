<details markdown="1">
  <summary>a little digression</summary>

In early roleplaying games in the D&D tradition, it would be common to fill dungeons up with well-hidden treasure and nasty little traps, and the players would be expected to describe quite explicitly how they go about searching for them. For example, in some version of the original D&D rules (quoted in <cite>The Elusive Shift</cite> by Jon Peterson), the example of play has the players searching a room:

> By way of ascertaining what the room looks like, the caller sets the party to work with statements such as “We’re examining the walls, ceiling, floor, and contents of the room itself.” The referee then provides a detailed description of the gnolls’ lair, and through a process of steady inquiry into the details of the space, all phrased as specific actions such as “Each trunk will be opened by one of us” and then “Check the trunk for secret drawers or a false bottom,” the intrepid caller uncovers hidden plunder.

Dungeons such as the infamous <cite>Tomb of Horrors</cite> introduced a variety of convoluted punishments to catch out even experienced dungeoneers (the statue with the Sphere of Annihilation for example), with a degree of sadism becoming the norm at least in that kind of convention play. Of course, taken to an extreme, defeating this would slow the game down to a crawl, as the players would learn that the only sensible way to proceed would be to check every square inch of the dungeon for traps, hazards and secret doors.

Since that's probably undesirable, later versions of dungeon-based games might choose to abstract over the process of searching with dice rolls etc., or just put the expectation on the GM to more or less overtly signpost in their narration when there might be something to be found. (If I want to flag the possibility of a secret door to my players, I might have another character disappear inside the room, or mention some markings on the floor...)

I don't know if anyone ever proposed a 'Knox's rules' for dungeon exploration, come to think of it. There are already so many differences of opinion on how best to do it... but nerds do love their lists of rules.

On that subject, mysteries are generally difficult to handle in RPGs, because there exists the possibility that players might simply miss a vital clue and never solve the mystery. The available solutions are all pretty awkward. For example, if you guarantee that the players will get the relevant information with enough abstracted dice rolls, then investigation becomes trivialised as a part of the game.

One particularly odd approach used in games like <cite>Brindlewood Bay</cite> is that there is no predefined solution, and instead the players get to decide what the solution to the mystery is. This sounds immensely unsatisfying, but I'll admit to having never played this family of games.

Perhaps the real solution is to rely on human flexibility. If the players fail to pick up on your planned mystery, you have the option to adjust the story to better signpost it if you think it appropriate, or advance the scheme they failed to notice. It's not so cut and dry as mandating 'the players will always solve the mystery'---it's part of a dynamic back-and-forth to give the players a puzzle that is *just hard enough* to be challenging to solve...

Battler is rather troubled by Knox's rules. Being able to rule out secret doors based on genre conventions, he argues, is pretty structurally similar to the magic stories presented so far.

Dlanor argues that allowing arbitrary unknown hidden doors would undermine the whole integrity of a locked-room mystery. Basically, it is a fully general solution, which trivialises the puzzle. After a minute, Battler accepts this logic.

But of course the perversity comes when the players are *aware* that they're in a Knox-obeying mystery, and they can start saying 'well, the detective doesn't need to investigate further because they would necessarily have found all relevant clues'.

<span class="name smallcaps">Virgilia</span> suggests this reflects a more general principle, that people are reluctant to solve problems without the guarantee of a solution.
